Why are my Facebook ads getting leads but no sales?

Cheap leads that never book usually aren't an ads problem. Here's where the journey actually breaks, and what to check first.

Andy MooreFounder, Ads Yeti
Updated 28 September 2026
9 min read

⚡ The short answer

Usually because the problem isn't the ads. When Meta is delivering leads but nobody's buying, the break is almost always somewhere after the click: the offer attracts the wrong people, the form is too easy, follow-up is too slow, or nobody's nurturing the lead until they're ready. Check what happens in the first hour after an enquiry before you touch the campaign.

Key takeaways

  • Cheap leads that don't buy are a journey problem more often than a targeting problem.
  • Speed to lead matters enormously. Look at how long it takes someone to call a new enquiry back.
  • Adding a little friction to the form (one qualifying question) usually improves lead quality.
  • Send your sales data back to Meta so it optimises for buyers, not form-fillers.
  • Change one thing at a time and follow it through to revenue, not just cost per lead.

I've lost count of the number of founders who've said some version of this to me: "The ads are working, we're getting loads of leads. We just can't get any of them to buy."

And I get it, because I've been there with my own business. Ads Manager looks brilliant. Cost per lead is lovely. Meanwhile the sales team is ringing people who either don't pick up or can't remember filling anything in. So where are the bloody bookings?

Here's the thing I keep coming back to: your customers don't live inside Ads Manager. The ad is only the first step of the journey. If the sales aren't happening, you need to follow the lead through every step after the click and find where it's falling over.

Where the journey usually breaks

When we pull apart an account with this problem, the break is almost always in one (or more) of five places:

  1. The offer attracts the wrong people. "Free guide" or "win a…" pulls in freebie hunters. Useful for list building, not so useful if you need people ready to spend.
  2. The form is too easy. Meta's instant forms pre-fill everything. Someone can become a "lead" in two taps without really meaning to.
  3. Follow-up is too slow. If a lead waits until tomorrow afternoon for a call, they've moved on, or they've spoken to someone else.
  4. There's no nurture. Plenty of good leads aren't ready today. Someone dreaming about a trek next spring won't book this week. If the only follow-up is two phone calls, you lose everyone who'd have booked next month.
  5. Meta is optimising for the wrong thing. If you tell it you want leads, it finds people who fill in forms. It has no idea which of them actually bought.

Only one of those five is really about the ads themselves. That's why tweaking audiences and creative often doesn't fix it.

What to check first (before you touch the campaign)

Take the last 50 or so leads and follow each one through. It's tedious, but it tells you more than any dashboard will. For each lead, note down:

  • How long before someone contacted them?
  • How many attempts were made, and on which channels?
  • Did they have an actual conversation?
  • If they did, why didn't they buy? (Price, timing, wrong fit, just browsing?)

One batch of leads is only one batch. If something looks odd, check it against another week before you rebuild everything around it.

Fix the form: add a bit of friction

This sounds backwards, because making it harder to enquire should mean fewer leads. It does. It also tends to mean better ones.

A couple of easy wins:

  • Switch the instant form to the higher intent type, which adds a review screen before submitting.
  • Add one qualifying question that a genuine buyer answers easily: budget range, timeframe, or what they're looking for.
  • Say clearly what happens next ("We'll call you within the hour") so people expect the call.

Speed to lead: the unglamorous fix that works

If I had to pick one thing to check first, it'd be this. A lead is at its warmest in the minutes after they've filled in the form. Every hour you wait, they cool off.

You don't need a call centre to fix it. A simple setup looks like:

  1. Lead comes in and lands in your CRM straight away (not a spreadsheet someone checks on Fridays).
  2. An instant text and email go out: thanks, here's who'll be in touch and when.
  3. The right person gets a notification on their phone.
  4. If there's no answer, there's a planned sequence of attempts over the next few days.

Nurture the ones who aren't ready yet

Some of your leads are perfectly good customers. They're just not ready this week. If your process gives up after two unanswered calls, they end up buying from whoever's still in their inbox when they are ready.

This is where content earns its keep. Answering the questions people ask before they buy (price, comparisons, the problems they're worried about) gives your nurture emails something useful to say, rather than "just checking in".

Tell Meta who actually bought

Out of the box, Meta only knows someone filled in a form. It has no idea whether they became a customer. So it keeps finding more form-fillers.

When you send your sales (or at least your qualified leads) back to Meta, through the Conversions API or offline conversion uploads from your CRM, it can start optimising for people who look like buyers. It's a bit of setup work, but it's often the single biggest change to lead quality over time.

Once it's in place, judge the campaign on cost per sale or cost per qualified lead, not cost per lead. A lead that costs twice as much but buys three times as often is a great deal.

What I'd do this week

If this sounds familiar, don't rebuild the whole account. Change as little as possible and test:

  1. Follow the last 50 leads through and find the biggest drop-off.
  2. Fix that one thing (usually speed to lead or the form).
  3. Give it a couple of weeks and compare cost per sale, not cost per lead.
  4. Then look at nurture and sending sales data back to Meta.

Right, go and have a look at what happened to last week's leads. I'd put money on the answer being more interesting than anything in Ads Manager.

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal for Facebook leads to be low quality?

It's common, but it isn't inevitable. Meta finds exactly who you ask it to find. If you optimise for the cheapest possible form fill, that's what you get. Qualifying questions, a clearer offer and feeding sales data back into Meta all push quality up.

Should I switch from Meta lead forms to a landing page?

Not automatically. Instant forms are fast and cheap, which is exactly why they can attract low-intent people. Try a 'higher intent' form type with a review step and a qualifying question first. If quality still isn't there, test a landing page against it and compare cost per sale, not cost per lead.

How quickly should we follow up a new lead?

As fast as you realistically can, ideally within minutes while they still remember filling in the form. If that's not possible, an instant automated text or email that sets expectations buys you time.

How do I know if it's the ads or the sales process?

Follow a batch of leads all the way through. How many were contacted, how many answered, how many had a real conversation, how many bought? If lots of leads never get a proper conversation, it's the process. If they get the conversation and they're simply the wrong people, look at the ad and the offer.
